# Complementary tools for scenes and sequences

These tools support the BMOC lens. They are not all Russell teachings.

## Primary craft sources

- **John August, [How to write a scene](https://johnaugust.com/2007/write-scene), 2007.** Start with the scene's necessary contribution and test what would break if it were omitted. Consider participants, setting, length, entry, and alternatives. Application: keep the scene's purpose in view so improving a local contest does not damage the film.
- **John August and Craig Mazin, [Scriptnotes 240](https://johnaugust.com/2016/scriptnotes-ep-240-david-mamet-and-the-producer-pass-transcript), 2016, discussing David Mamet.** Connect immediate wants, consequences of failure, and urgency to the dramatic encounter. Application: specify what the character needs from the other person now, not only their life ambition.

The diagnostics and examples below are this skill's practical synthesis. They do not establish a universal formula or predict audience retention.

## Escalation test

For a proposed escalation, finish the sentence: “Because of this action or response, the character can no longer ___ without ___.” If nothing concrete changes, check whether the proposal merely repeats the demand or increases volume.

Possible changes: a private request becomes witnessed; a concession commits resources; an ally's cooperation costs trust; a failed bluff proves the opponent has leverage; success produces responsibility; the only remaining route conflicts with a value. Use established facts. Additions need setup and approval when they change canon.

Separate rising pressure from constant worsening. A clear success can be necessary and satisfying. Its consequences may open the next phase without cancelling the victory.

## Audience information and endings

Track three columns internally: audience knows / character knows / unresolved question. Suspense needs enough orientation to anticipate a consequential outcome. Mystery can withhold an answer while keeping the immediate action legible. Surprise should survive a retrospective logic check.

For an interrupted contest, retain the live question across the cut. On return, pay it off or give a meaningful advancement. For an omitted climactic action, supply sufficient setup and aftermath to make the causal and emotional outcome legible. These are practical safeguards; do not claim an exact Russell definition unless the source supports it.

A scene exit can propel action or deepen feeling. A held look after a choice may be stronger than a new danger. The useful test is what the audience carries forward, not whether every scene ends with an unanswered threat.

## Sequence consequence chain

Use a short table: scene / local question / changed state / consequence for the next scene. Separate parallel storylines and identify their point of convergence or contrast. For a causal sequence, a rearrangement test reveals interchangeable encounters. For montage or associative work, test the intended accumulation instead.

Ask where cost is carried, where the protagonist changes approach, where an apparent success changes the problem, and where the sequence question receives its decisive answer. Do not force the sequence's B/M/O/C onto each constituent scene.

## Intervention size and the opponent's reply

Choose retain, clarify, compress, change tactic, or redesign based on the diagnosed cause. Prefer deletion when two encounters produce the same result. A fresh location is not a new dramatic function. Before adding a condition to justify an extra scene, test whether the extra scene should exist.

A condition should express the opponent's pursuit. A reluctant collaborator may need a visible concession, a share of control, or a credible limit on risk; use only what their established motives support. Work out what they can actually know and enforce. Making a clever offer does not guarantee that a competent opponent accepts it.

A consequence is not automatically escalation. “They agree to discuss it elsewhere” can be a practical bridge yet add no emotional or strategic pressure. Identify the changed exposure, cost, commitment, or uncertainty. Otherwise compress the bridge.

## Audience involvement beyond Yes and No

For suspense, locate the audience's fear in a foreseeable event. Decide which information must be shown for that fear to exist. Check each subsequent action against who knows what, physical access, travel, visibility, and time. A hidden action can be fair; making a viewpoint character behave unnaturally to hide their own knowledge usually is not. Do not solve a scene by inventing a technological capability or procedural rule that the story has not earned.

For comedy, follow a character's intelligible attempt to preserve status, dignity, a lie, or control. Let the attempted solution create the next exposure or demand. Increasing embarrassment can be escalation without greater physical stakes. Track the audience's advantage in knowledge and allow the game to change; a string of unrelated mishaps is not an escalating comic mechanism.

For intimacy, track permission, avoidance, reciprocity, or the changed meaning of an action. More explicit staging may clarify a choice but remove ambiguity or actor freedom. Test whether the existing gesture already lands before adding a second proof of it.

## Quiet-scene counterexample

After a funeral, two siblings wash cups. One puts their mother's chipped cup in a donation box; the other silently retrieves it. The first moves the box aside and dries the cup. This can move from solitary protection of grief to tacit shared care without a deadline, secret, argument, or four separate crescendos. Read the surrounding pages before deciding whether this earns its duration. The cup's handling is evidence; a childhood wound is not established.
