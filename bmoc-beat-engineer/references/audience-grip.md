# Audience grip: complementary lenses for scenes and sequences

These lenses come from other writers and from suspense research. None of them is Peter Russell's teaching. Use them when a scene is clear but still fails to hold attention, or when the writer asks why something drags. Pick the one or two that explain the actual weakness; do not run them as a checklist. Research checked 2026-10-13.

## 1. The value turn and the non-event

Name the value at stake in the scene (trust, safety, freedom, love, status, truth) and mark its charge at the start and at the end. If the charge is the same, the scene is a non-event, usually exposition with furniture. A scene can answer its local question and still change nothing in the character's life; this test catches that. Source: [McKee, "Do Your Scenes Turn?"](https://mckeestory.com/do-your-scenes-turn/)

## 2. The gap and no retreat

Each action is taken expecting a result; drama lives in the gap between what the character expected and what the world gives back. A beat whose result matches the expectation is flat. Across a scene or sequence, actions must grow bolder or costlier; a smaller action after a bigger one, or a tactic retried after it failed, makes the middle sag. Sources: McKee, *Story* (1997); summary at [Susannah Waters, "Keep Climbing"](https://susannahwaters.com/tips/keep-climbing).

## 3. The forced choice

The climax of a unit means most when it follows a crisis: a choice between two bad options (the best bad choice) or two goods that cannot both be kept. If the right answer is obvious, there is no crisis. Turning points come by action (someone does something) or by revelation (information lands); mix both and alternate the direction of value shifts across a sequence. Hold information back until it can turn a scene. Sources: [Story Grid, 5 Commandments](https://storygrid.com/5-commandments-storytelling-revisited/), [Story Grid on revelation turning points](https://storygrid.com/2200-2/).

## 4. Who knows what

The same events produce different effects depending on order:
- **Suspense:** show the threat before the outcome. Hitchcock's bomb under the table turns small talk into dread once the audience knows the bomb is there.
- **Curiosity:** show the outcome first, then how it happened.
- **Surprise:** hide a key fact, then reveal it. A few seconds of shock instead of minutes of suspense.

Dramatic irony (the audience knows what a character doesn't) works in every genre, comedy included, and runs in three stages: preparation, suspension, resolution. Too short a suspension does nothing; too long goes slack. Often the cheapest repair is a reorder, not a rewrite. Sources: [No Film School on Hitchcock and Truffaut](https://nofilmschool.com/alfred-hitchcock-and-francois-truffaut-explain-surprise-vs-suspense); Yves Lavandier, *Writing Drama*; Brewer and Lichtenstein's structural-affect work, summarized at [MovieMaker](https://www.moviemaker.com/first-draft-psychological-states/).

## 5. What the research says drives suspense

- Suspense needs concern, not only uncertainty: a character we care about facing a likely bad outcome. [Kaspar et al. 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01913/pdf)
- Suspense rose when the hero's escape routes narrowed, even when readers' estimate of survival barely changed. Gerrig and Bernardo, 1994.
- Suspense tracks how far the audience's beliefs could swing in the next moment. If the next beat cannot move the outcome much either way, it feels predictable. Ely, Frankel and Kamenica, 2015; Li, Bramley and Gureckis, 2019.
- Curiosity spikes when people know part of something and want the rest; a partial clue beats a total mystery. The popular claim that people remember unfinished things better did not hold up in a 2025 meta-analysis, but the urge to return to an interrupted task did. [Ghibellini and Meier 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html)

Practical reading: make us care about the person at risk, then remove exits one at a time, and make sure each beat could swing the outcome.

## 6. Sequences and anticipation across cuts

A feature can be read as roughly eight sequences of 10-15 minutes, each a short film with its own tension that only partly resolves, so what remains open carries the audience into the next. Gulino names four tools of anticipation: telegraphing (an announced event or deadline), the dangling cause (a cause set up, effect held back), dramatic irony, and open dramatic tension (will they get what they want). At every cut at least one should be live. Sources: [The Story Department on the sequence approach](https://www.thestorydepartment.com/the-sequence-approach), [Gulino at Film Courage](https://filmcourage.com/2019/05/24/how-to-keep-the-audience-in-anticipation-by-chapman-university-professor-paul-joseph-gulino/).

## 7. Why now, and lines that only inform

Every scene answers: who wants what, what happens if they don't get it, and why now. If "why now" has no answer, the scene can float anywhere and usually should be cut or moved. Watch for characters talking about someone who isn't there and lines that exist only to inform the audience. Source: [Mamet's memo to the writers of The Unit](https://www.slashfilm.com/a-letter-from-david-mamet-to-the-writers-of-the-unit/).

## 8. In late, out early

Cut arrivals, greetings and wind-downs. Enter at the last possible moment and leave once the goal is reached or blocked, ideally on an open question. Exceptions: a deliberate breather at the start of a sequence or act, and endings that need their silence. Source: [The Story Department](https://www.thestorydepartment.com/screenwriting-in-late-out-early/).

## 9. Endings that pull forward

Act-outs and scene buttons come in types: rest, exclamation, question, trail-off, abrupt cut. Cliffhanger devices include the bombshell, the confrontation, the unexpected or withheld response, and dramatic irony. Vary them; three of the same in a row wear out. In streaming, Netflix reported that viewers got "hooked" on a series around episodes 2-4, not the pilot, so the pilot's job is a promise that pays off soon. Sources: [Creative Screenwriting on cliffhangers](https://www.creativescreenwriting.com/cswcms/10-ways-to-write-nail-biting-cliffhangers/), [Netflix "hooked" study](http://about.netflix.com/en/news/do-you-know-when-you-were-hooked-netflix-does).

## 10. Quiet scenes: tension inside the line

In a stretch without external conflict, tension can live inside a single moment: what the character feels pulling against what they say or do. Read three random lines; each should carry some friction. This protects quiet scenes from being "fixed" with invented confrontation. Source: Donald Maass on micro-tension, summarized at [Live Write Thrive](https://www.livewritethrive.com/2025/11/17/microtension-a-must-in-your-fiction/).

Status is a related axis for dialogue and comedy: who is up at the start, who at the end, and does it move? Source: Keith Johnstone, *Impro*, summarized at [James Clear](https://jamesclear.com/book-summaries/impro).
